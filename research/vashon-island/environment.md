# Vashon Island — Geography, Ecology & Climate

## Geology

### Glacial Origins
Vashon Island was formed by the retreat of the Vashon Stade of the Fraser Glaciation, the last major ice age in the Puget Sound region (~12,000–10,000 BCE). The island's terrain is composed of glacial till — a mix of clay, sand, gravel, and boulders deposited by the retreating ice sheet. The glaciation carved the deep waterways of Puget Sound that now surround the island.

### Stratigraphy
The retreating Vashon glacier left a distinctive layered geological profile:
- **Vashon Till** (surface): Dense, unsorted mixture of clay, silt, sand, and gravel
- **Esperance Sand** (middle): Porous advance outwash deposited by meltwater streams ahead of the glacier
- **Lawton Clay** (deep): Fine sediment deposited in proglacial lakes, largely impermeable

This layering determines the island's hydrology: water percolates through permeable till and outwash until hitting impermeable clay, emerging as springs and seeps along coastal bluffs. About 80% of the island's drinking water today comes from the Vashon Advance Outwash aquifer.

### Puget Sound Formation
By approximately 14,000 BCE, glacial retreat opened the Strait of Juan de Fuca, allowing saltwater to re-enter the Puget Sound basin and transforming freshwater proglacial lakes (including Glacial Lake Russell) into a saltwater inland sea. The resulting fjord system averages 450 feet in depth, with a maximum of 930 feet off Jefferson Point. Deep channels surrounding Vashon Island — some as deep as the island is high (over 600 feet) — were carved by subglacial meltwater during the Vashon Stade.

**Related entries:** pre-003

### Terrain
- **Bluffs:** Steep glacial bluffs line much of the coastline, particularly on the western and northern shores. Formed by erosion exploiting the contrast between permeable and impermeable geological layers.
- **Ravines:** Hundreds of small ravines and creek valleys carved through erodible glacial sediments by surface water. Shinglemill Creek and Judd Creek cut entirely through the outwash layer.
- **Soils:** Rich glacial soils support agriculture, particularly the clay loam ideal for strawberry cultivation. The clay deposits at Quartermaster Harbor also supported a significant brick-making industry (1880s–1890s).
- **Elevation:** Rolling terrain with moderate elevation; highest point approximately 450 feet above sea level.

## Ecology

### Post-Glacial Succession (~12,000–8,000 BCE)
After ice sheet retreat, the bare glacial landscape underwent classic post-glacial ecological succession:
1. **16,000–15,000 BP:** Temperatures ~4°C colder than present; largely barren landscape
2. **14,000–12,000 BP:** Lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*) established in open parkland conditions
3. **12,000–10,000 BP:** Wider variety of trees; transition to closed forest with western hemlock and alder
4. **~8,000 BCE:** Signature Pacific Northwest species (Douglas fir, western red cedar, western hemlock) dominate; old-growth temperate rainforest established

The entire transition from ice-covered landscape to closed forest took approximately 5,000–7,000 years.

**Related entries:** pre-005

### Historic Forest Cover
Before European-American settlement, the island was densely forested with old-growth:
- **Douglas fir** (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*) — dominant canopy species
- **Western red cedar** (*Thuja plicata*) — culturally significant to Coast Salish peoples; called the "Tree of Life"
- **Western hemlock** (*Tsuga heterophylla*) — shade-tolerant understory
- **Big-leaf maple** (*Acer macrophyllum*) — in ravines and lowlands
- **Red alder** (*Alnus rubra*) — pioneer species in disturbed areas

The cedar was the foundation of Coast Salish material culture — used for longhouses (some hundreds of feet long), dugout canoes, clothing, mats, ropes, and baskets. The inner bark was stripped and pounded into pliable material for weaving.

**Related entries:** ind-004, log-001

### Deforestation & Recovery
- **1852–1860s:** Early logging camps near shoreline; ox-team skid roads to beach. An estimated 200 million board feet of timber on island as of mid-1880s.
- **1893:** Major wildfire burned logged-off areas near Center for two days; nearly reached town before wind shift.
- **1910–1915:** Puget Mill Company cleared roughly 30% of the island's land area (10 square miles) in industrial-scale operations. A 1922 blaze burned the width of the south end for two miles.
- **1994:** WSU Extension forestry assessment found over 60% of the island had been reforested with second-growth timber.
- **Present:** A few surviving old-growth trees remain in the deepest ravines.

**Related entries:** log-001, log-004, pio-013, gro-006, ear-006

### Marine Environment
- **Shoreline:** Mix of rocky beaches, sandy shores, and tidal flats. Vashon carries half of all King County's shorelines.
- **Salmon:** All five species of Pacific salmon (Chinook, coho, sockeye, chum, pink) colonized Puget Sound ~14,900 years ago in two pulses — from the south via the Chehalis River and from the north through the Strait of Juan de Fuca.
- **Shellfish:** Productive intertidal beds established by ~6,000 BCE, particularly in Quartermaster Harbor. Native species include littleneck clams, butter clams, horse clams, and native Olympia oysters. Indigenous peoples constructed rock-walled terraces (clam gardens) as a form of ancient mariculture.
- **Eelgrass beds:** Nearshore eelgrass provides critical habitat for juvenile fish and invertebrates.
- **Marine mammals:** Harbor seals; endangered Southern Resident orca whales in surrounding waters.
- **Herring, smelt, and anchovies:** Important components of the marine food web and indigenous diet, alongside the better-known salmon.

### Agricultural Landscape
Five distinct phases of agricultural history:
1. **Indigenous stewardship** (pre-contact): Seasonal round of resource gathering — camas root, berries (salal, huckleberry, salmonberry), shellfish, salmon, waterfowl, marine mammals. Fire used to maintain prairie openings.
2. **Homestead era** (1865–1890s): Mixed subsistence farming; clearing forest for fields.
3. **Strawberry era** (1890–1942): Signature crop cultivated primarily by Japanese American families. Mukai cold-process barreling innovation (1926). Harrington-Beall Greenhouse Company (est. ~1888) grew roses and orchids commercially.
4. **Post-internment decline** (1942–1970s): Loss of Japanese American farming expertise and labor; agricultural collapse. Land transitioned to other uses.
5. **Organic revival** (1980s–present): Vashon Island Growers Association (VIGA) formed mid-1980s. Farmers Market established at Village Green. Sustainable and organic farming emphasis. VIGA developed horticulture curriculum at Vashon High School.

**Related entries:** ind-005, gro-001, gro-009, ear-001, ear-004, ear-012, ww2-007, mod-005

### Conservation
- **Vashon-Maury Island Land Trust** (founded 1989): Over 2,000 acres permanently protected, 10 miles of public trails. Preserves include Shinglemill Creek, Judd Creek, Fisher Pond, Christensen Pond, and 12 acres of the historic Matsuda Farm. Programs include "Seven over Seventy" (large private holdings) and "The Four Creeks Campaign" (major watersheds).
- **Glacier Northwest Mine Controversy** (1998–2010): Proposed gravel mine expansion on Maury Island (7.5M tons/year) fought by Preserve Our Islands. Concerns: arsenic contamination, orca habitat, aquatic reserve. Blocked by federal court in 2009. King County purchased site for $36M in 2010; became county park with 200+ acres of madrone forest.
- **Whispering Firs Bog:** Fragile wetland ecosystem, the Land Trust's founding conservation project.

**Related entries:** mod-006, mod-008

## Climate

### Pacific Maritime
Vashon Island has a mild, maritime climate moderated by the waters of Puget Sound:
- **Winters:** Cool and wet (average January temperature ~40°F / 4°C)
- **Summers:** Warm and dry (average July temperature ~65°F / 18°C)
- **Precipitation:** ~38 inches annually, concentrated October through March
- **Rain shadow:** Partially in the Olympic rain shadow, receiving less rain than areas to the west
- **Frost:** Occasional; the maritime influence moderates temperature extremes
- **Growing season:** Approximately 200–250 days, favorable for agriculture

### Holocene Climatic Optimum (~7,500–2,500 BCE)
A prolonged warm period with temperatures averaging ~2°C warmer than the modern baseline and ~45% less precipitation. This expanded prairie-like openings and oak woodland at the expense of dense conifer forest, influenced fire regimes, and affected distribution of plant foods important to early inhabitants. The modern cool, wet climate pattern became established after ~4,200 years ago.
